Lesson 2

One-time vs recurring payments

Buying something once and paying every month look similar to the customer, but they're built very differently. Recurring payments need the customer's permission to take money later, when they're not around.

One-time payments (orders)

The simple case. You create an order for a fixed amount, the customer pays once and it's done. Examples: buying an add-on, unlocking one extra report, a one-off fee.

  1. Your backend asks the gateway: "create an order for ₹999 for this customer".
  2. The gateway returns a session ID.
  3. The customer pays on the gateway's checkout.
  4. You confirm the order is PAID, then deliver.

Once paid, the gateway has no permission to charge this customer again.

Recurring payments and mandates

For a monthly subscription, you need to take money every month without the customer opening your site and approving each time. That permission is called a mandate.

A mandate is like the form you sign at a gym: "you may deduct ₹499 from my account every month until I cancel". You sign it once. After that, the gym collects automatically, and you can cancel it any time.

With a gateway, the customer "signs" the mandate on the checkout page by approving it with their UPI PIN, card OTP or bank login. After that, the gateway charges on schedule and tells you each time through a webhook.

Types of mandates in India

MandateCustomer approves withHow fast it's activeNotes

Why eNACH is special

With UPI and cards, approval is final the moment the customer finishes checkout. With eNACH, the customer finishes, but the bank still has to confirm the mandate. The subscription sits in a "waiting for bank" state, often called BANK_APPROVAL_PENDING. Your app must decide what to do in that gap: give access now, or wait.

The rules: RBI recurring payment guidelines

In India, the RBI has rules for automatic debits. The gateway handles most of them for you, but you should know them:

  • Pre-debit notification. The customer must be told before each automatic debit (at least 24 hours before). The gateway sends this by SMS or email. You just choose the channels.
  • Extra approval above a limit. Automatic debits up to a set amount (₹15,000 for most categories, higher for some such as mutual funds, insurance premiums and credit card bills) go through without extra approval. Above that, the customer must approve each debit.
  • Easy cancellation. The customer can cancel a mandate any time, from their UPI app or bank, without asking you. Your system must handle cancellations that come from outside.
Limits change

These amounts have been revised several times. Check the current RBI circular and your gateway's docs before relying on a number.

Plans

A plan is the template for a subscription: what to charge and how often. Think of the Netflix plans: Basic, Standard, Premium. Each customer's subscription points to one plan.

Two kinds of plan

Periodic

Fixed amount on a fixed schedule. The gateway charges automatically. "₹499 every month."

On-demand

No schedule. You call the API to charge, any amount up to a maximum. Good for usage-based billing.

Where plans come from

You can create plans in two ways, and both give the same result:

  • Dashboard: fill a form in the gateway's website.
  • API: one call (e.g. POST /plans) from a small setup script.

Either way it's a one-time setup per environment: once in sandbox and once in production. It isn't something customers trigger, so it doesn't need retry protection. If the call fails, check whether the plan exists and run it again.

Some gateways (Cashfree included) also let you skip separate plans and describe the plan inline inside each "create subscription" request.

Edge cases with plans
  • Plans usually can't be edited. To change a price, create a new plan (starter_monthly_v2) and point new subscriptions at it. Existing subscribers stay on the old price unless you move them.
  • The price is defined in two places. If your code says ₹499 but the gateway's plan says ₹599, the customer is charged ₹599 while your database records ₹499. Keep one source of truth: either build plans inline from your config, or check at startup that they match.
  • Max amount is a hard cap. If you later raise the price above it, existing mandates can't charge the new amount. The customer must approve a new mandate.

The response gives you the gateway's subscription ID, a status (INITIALIZED at first: nothing approved yet) and a session ID to open checkout.

The first-month trap

Two settings decide when the customer pays for the first month, and they interact badly if you don't think about them together:

  • Auth amount: money taken when the customer approves the mandate. It can be a token amount (₹1, often refunded) or a full month's price.
  • First charge time: when the first scheduled charge runs.
SetupWhat happensResult
Auth = full month, first charge = nowCustomer pays at approval, and again right awayDouble charge
Auth = full month, first charge = +1 monthThe approval payment is month 1Correct
Auth = token (refunded), first charge = nowToken taken and returned; month 1 charged immediatelyCorrect
Auth = token, first charge = +1 monthNobody paid for month 1Free month
eNACH breaks the pattern

eNACH can't take money at approval: the auth amount is effectively ₹0. If your design relies on "auth amount = month 1", eNACH users get month 1 free. Either set the first charge to happen immediately for eNACH, or accept it as a known gap. Test every payment method in sandbox and check exactly when each charge happens.

The subscription lifecycle

A subscription moves through states. Exact names vary by gateway; these are Cashfree's, and others are similar.

Edge case: a failed new attempt must not remove old access

A customer with active access starts checkout for a different plan, then abandons it. That new subscription becomes LINK_EXPIRED. If your code reacts by setting the user to "no access", you've just taken away something they paid for. Only change a user's access based on the subscription that actually granted it.

Tip: separate "payment state" from "access"

The subscription status says what the mandate is doing. Access should come from what was paid for: usually "paid until expiryDate". A cancelled subscription can still give access until its paid period ends.

AUTH vs CHARGE payments

One subscription produces many individual payments over its life:

AUTH payment

Happens once, when the customer approves the mandate. It may be ₹0 (eNACH), a token amount, or a full month.

CHARGE payment

Every scheduled debit after that: month 2, month 3… Each one can succeed or fail on its own.

Keep a payments ledger: one row per actual payment, keyed by the gateway's payment ID, separate from the subscription row. That gives you receipts, a history, and protection against counting the same payment twice.
